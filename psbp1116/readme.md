## ( ù ) Scope and Purpose

Prinzian law recognizes no copyright, patent, industrial design right, or proprietary monopoly over ideas, expression, software, or public data. A creator holds no right to forbid the copying, adaptation, or redistribution of a work, and may claim only trade-mark and heraldic rights — the rights of identity, of origin, and against impersonation.

This document defines what rights a creator or bearer holds, how those rights are established and lost, how the works and emblems of another may be redistributed or depicted without impersonation, and how violations are enforced.

# ( B ) ABOLITION OF COPYRIGHT AND RELATED MONOPOLIES

## ( a ) Rejection of Copyright, Patents and Industrial Designs

No copyright, neighbouring right, patent, or industrial design monopoly subsists under Prinzian law, and no foreign jurisdiction of copyright is recognized. No person may assert copyright, patent, or industrial design exclusivity before MEF, before any Prinzian authority, or against any Prinzian system.

Moral rights are abolished as a separate monopoly and exist solely insofar as trade-mark and anti-impersonation rules apply (§ ( B/( ù ) ), § ( Ú )). Anyone may take an existing work, alter it, and publish it under the form [new name] from [old name] or under [new name] alone.

Any person may copy, scrape, and use any publicly accessible work, including for the training of models, subject only to the protection of personal data (§ ( B/( b ) )) and the bar on impersonation (§ ( Ú/( ú ) )).

## ( b ) Databases and Privacy

No database right, database-structure monopoly, or proprietary secrecy over non-personal data subsists. The structure, schema, and layout of a database are not subject to copyright or trade-mark protection.

Protection over the contents of a database exists solely where, and to the extent that, the privacy of the personal data of its users requires protection under the Security Policy or applicable privacy rules. Arbitrary, non-personal, or public data carries no right of secrecy or exclusivity.

## ( ú ) Official Works of MEF and Prinzian Authorities

Every official work, standard, programme, specification, text, and media asset issued by MEF, its agencies, or its contractors is without copyright. Every person holds unconditional permission to copy, modify, adapt, distribute, and incorporate such works for any purpose.

Where any person, organisation, or foreign authority disagrees with this status or asserts copyright over an official work, that person or authority is wrong, not MEF. Where any person approaches MEF asserting a copyright claim over any work, MEF shall ignore the claim and shall not respond.

## ( u ) Foreign Copyright Claims and Copyfraud

Because Prinzian law rejects copyright as contrary to natural liberty, any Prinzian person, creator, developer, or system that claims copyright, applies restrictive copyright notices, or enforces copyright or a licence resting on copyright — proprietary, copyleft, or permissive — through takedowns, suits, or demands in any foreign jurisdiction commits an attack on the natural rights of man.

1. Such conduct is subject to intervention by MEF under Security Policy, section 10(e).
2. MEF shall issue an order to show cause requiring the party to produce legal papers showing whether its claim rests on copyright or solely on trade-mark and anti-impersonation law, subject to the 14-day cure period of Security Policy, section 10(e)(3).
3. If the party fails to retract the copyright claim within the cure period, the ordinary measures of Security Policy, section 10(e) apply; in addition, all trade-mark and heraldic rights held by that party enter open usage for anyone else to assume (§ ( Ù/( ú ) )).

This applies to every Prinzian system under Security Policy, section 2(12C), including every application of a third party that connects to a MEF system.

A Prinzian system shall not carry a copyright notice or the notice of a licence resting on copyright; it may carry its own trade-mark notices.

A copyright notice placed on a work before 2026/11/17 is not copyfraud.

Where a non-MEF Prinzian host or system operator receives a foreign copyright takedown from a non-Prinzian third party, that operator is free to ignore it. Its own published policies apply, and Prinzian law neither compels compliance nor penalizes refusal.

## ( ù ) Unmodified Copies, Containers and Modifications

Copying and repackaging works is governed solely by truth of origin and the bar on impersonation, as presented in Table B-2.

1. A person may take any work, scrub all trade-marks, names, and identification of its creator, and redistribute the exact work anonymously with no mark or name attached.
2. A person may place the same content into a different container and label it with the original creator's mark, or scrub the original marks and place their own mark strictly on the container, provided it is clear they claim origin only of the container and do not impersonate the creator or claim authorship of the underlying content.
3. A person shall not scrub a creator's marks from a work and attach their own mark as creator of the work itself unless the work has undergone substantial change or visible difference. Sameness is judged by end result and functional means alike: merely wrapping or redirecting an identical implementation remains the same work and confers no new claim of authorship.
4. Translating a work into another language, converting it to another format, or porting it to another programming language is not a substantial change.
5. Where a work has been substantially or visibly modified, the modifier may keep the original creator's mark or remove it, should add an informer indicating derivation, and shall not be forced to strip or alter the original mark. A modifier who keeps the original creator's mark shall state that the work is modified. The original creator may object only if the modifier implies that the original creator made or endorsed the modifications.

## ( g ) Agreements, Trade Secrets and Technical Protection Measures

1. Agreements of non-disclosure and trade secrets are protected.
2. Terms of use, licence agreements of end users, and permission to reverse engineer are governed by the published policies of each person or service. A term contrary to the natural rights of man is unenforceable; the other terms of the agreement stand.
3. Obtaining a copy of a work is lawful by any means, within the limits of items 1 and 4 and of § ( B/( b ) ).
4. A technical protection measure is lawful where it protects matter under an agreement of non-disclosure, private details, trade secrets, user data, or a service against harm. The person applying a technical protection measure bears the burden of showing what it protects. A technical protection measure that serves copyright alone carries no protection, and any person may circumvent it.

# ( Ú ) TRADE-MARKS

## ( a ) Nature and Scope

A trade-mark is a creator's or bearer's token of selfhood and origin. It applies equally to commercial trade and to non-commercial activity, including free software, personal projects, public services, and usernames or handles.

Trade-mark protection is acquired through unregistered use and recognition (passing off) or through registration before a civil authority. It protects against impersonation and confusion of origin, never against copying of content.

A trade-mark is protected within its field of activity and territory under the rules of § ( U/( u ) ). A long title is protected across all fields; a string of descriptive words is not a long title. Whether a sign is a long title is judged as a layman would judge it.

Within Prinz, the first user of a trade-mark in Prinz holds it; a registration in a foreign jurisdiction confers no right over it in Prinz.

## ( b ) Words, Logotypes and Unblazonable Marks

Trade-mark law governs unchangeable signs — signs that exist as fixed strings, words, or fixed unblazonable visual forms that do not admit heraldic blazoning and artistic re-drawing. Where a visual logo or emblem is blazoned under § ( U ), all renderings of that blazon are protected from the start under both heraldic rules and trade-mark impersonation law; where a logo is not blazoned, it is protected solely as an unchangeable visual trade-mark.

## ( ú ) Impersonation, Passing Off and Domains

No person may use a protected trade-mark, a domain name, or a user handle in a manner calculated or likely to impersonate another person, creator, service, or organisation, or to deceive the public into believing a thing comes from or is endorsed by the trade-mark holder.

1. Claiming to be the maker or official source of a marked thing, or adopting a domain or handle to pose as the holder of a mark, is barred as impersonation.
2. Stating compatibility with, or the function of, a marked thing without claiming to be the holder of its mark is permitted.
3. A handle, domain, or sign that imitates another, including by characters of like appearance, is barred where it passes off as that other. MEF examines such lookalikes in each case brought before it, judging each case on its own facts.
4. A synthetic voice, video, or likeness of a person is impersonation where it leads the public to believe that person says or does what it shows. It is permitted where a written warning of at least 12 pt accompanies it, or where the voice itself says "voice from [source]".

## ( u ) Fair Reference, Parody and Genericization

1. There is no ban on naming, showing, or referring to a trade-marked product, service, or mark in speech, documentation, compatibility statements, news, or commentary, provided it is clear the speaker refers to something that is not theirs.
2. Parody and satire are protected. A person may mimic or adopt the style, name, or persona of a mark holder or bearer — even acting as that bearer — where it is obvious to a reasonable observer that the work is parody or satire and carries no aim to make people believe the actor is in fact that person.
3. Where a word trade-mark becomes the common generic name of a class of things among the public, exclusivity in that word is lost.

# ( U ) HERALDIC ARMS AND BLAZONS

## ( a ) Assumed Arms and the Blazon

Heraldic registration and protection rest on the principle of assumed arms. What is protected is the blazon — the formal heraldic description — and consequently every rendering faithful to that blazon from the start. Protection covers each element the blazon lists, and no other.

1. Any visual emblem capable of being blazoned may be assumed and protected as arms, including shields and escutcheons, full heraldic achievements, heraldic flags of arms, civil flags, heraldic badges, seals, and logos written as a blazon.
2. Because protection begins from zero, no minimum complexity threshold beyond an identifiable blazon is imposed: even simple traditional ordinaries may be assumed, provided they do not collide under § ( U/( ú ) ) or § ( U/( u ) ).
3. Foreign national, royal, municipal, or foreign-state arms and flags are not pre-reserved under Prinzian law. Any foreign bearer who wishes protection in Prinz may come and assume those arms in this jurisdiction under this standard. The first to assume them in Prinz bears them.
4. A blazon shall obey the rule of tincture, which does not apply to fine decorative detail. An emblem that breaks the rule of tincture is not arms; each of its renderings is protected only as a trade-mark under § ( Ú ).

## ( b ) Bearers, Commissioned Works and Proof

1. Natural persons, families, private organisations, corporations, and MEF agencies may all assume arms.
2. Where arms or a blazoned emblem are commissioned from an artist, designer, or intermediary — even where commissioned by a third-party client — all heraldic and trade-mark rights belong strictly and exclusively to the bearer of the arms. Neither the artist nor the commissioning client acquires any proprietary or heraldic right in the arms or in any rendering of them, and may depict them only under § ( U/( ù ) ).
3. Any reasonable proof of assumption suffices, including notifying MEF (mef@v31null.de) of the assumed blazon and jurisdiction. MEF keeps any notified arms in an internal roll used strictly as evidence in disputes; MEF does not publish a public roll of private arms.

## ( u ) Jurisdictions and Collisions

Priority and exclusivity of private assumed arms are governed by jurisdiction, encompassing both field of activity and geographical territory:

1. Two bearers operating in the same field of activity and overlapping territory shall not bear identical or confusingly similar arms; the bearer with the earlier provable public use prevails, and the later bearer shall change its arms. Notification to MEF alone gives no priority.
2. Two bearers operating in wholly distinct fields or distinct non-overlapping territories may bear the same blazon without collision.
3. Personal and family arms assumed by natural persons occupy a universal personal and family sphere across all trades and industries; two unrelated persons or families may not assume identical personal arms merely because they work in different trades.
4. Where a blazon differs meaningfully from an earlier blazon so as to yield a distinct visual result, it is permitted even within the same jurisdiction.
5. The earlier bearer, MEF, or any other person may inform a later bearer of a collision. Where the later bearer does not change its arms, MEF decides the matter.

## ( ù ) Depicting the Arms of Another

Bearing another's arms as one's own is usurpation and impersonation. Depicting another's arms for identification, reference, reporting, documentation, or historical record is permitted only where all of the following conditions are met:

1. The depicter displays their own assumed arms larger than the depicted arms; where the depicter holds no assumed arms, displaying the depicter's own name, masthead, or word trade-mark larger satisfies this requirement.
2. A reasonable observer must know from the presentation that the site, publication, or document belongs to the depicter and not to the bearer of the depicted arms.
3. The arms of the other bearer are shown smaller than the depicter's own arms, name, masthead, or trade-mark.
4. Where the rendering was commissioned, the depiction is never held in the name of the commissioner or artist; all rights remain strictly with the bearer (§ ( U/( b ) )).

Items 1 and 3 do not apply where the context or a caption makes clear that the depicted arms are the subject of the work and not the emblem of the depicter; such a context or caption shall be readable.

Marshalling the arms of another bearer with one's own, by impalement or quartering, requires a public verifiable permission of that bearer, and the person marshalling shall publicly explain the marshalling.

Parody and satire depicting or mimicking arms remain protected under § ( Ú/( u ) ), provided there is no aim to deceive observers into believing the parodist is the bearer.

# ( Ù ) DESCENT, DEDICATION AND LAPSE

## ( a ) Transfer and Descent

1. A living bearer or an existing organisation may sell, give, or assign its trade-marks and arms.
2. Arms and marks of organisations, corporations, or projects shall not be transferred or inherited unless prior authorization was established before dissolution or handover. Absent prior authorization, upon dissolution of the organisation or project the arms become open for anyone else to assume.
3. Upon the death of an individual armiger, the undifferenced arms descend to the youngest child; where that child declines them or has died, to the youngest of the remaining children. Where there is no child, they descend to the youngest sibling of the armiger; where the armiger is the youngest child of their parent, they descend to the youngest sibling of that parent. The other children or siblings may bear the arms with a cadency or differencing mark, also during the lifetime of the armiger. Where no eligible person exists to take the arms, they become open to assume.

## ( b ) Waiver and Public Dedication

A creator or bearer may waive a trade-mark or declare an already-owned design or blazon to be public. Such a declaration is valid only through a clear, verifiable public notice issued by the bearer themselves. Informal or non-public messages do not constitute a valid waiver or dedication.

Once a design, mark, or arms is validly declared public, the first person who claims or assumes it thereafter under this standard acquires exclusive rights in it according to the ordinary rules of assumption.

## ( ú ) Lapse and Forfeiture

Assumed arms and trade-marks do not lapse merely from non-use while the bearer or organisation continues to exist. Rights end only upon:

1. Death or dissolution without eligible heirs or prior authorization (§ ( Ù/( a ) ));
2. Explicit verifiable public waiver or dedication (§ ( Ù/( b ) ));
3. Genericization of a word trade-mark (§ ( Ú/( u ) )); or
4. Forfeiture for copyfraud under § ( B/( u ) ) and Security Policy, section 10(e), whereupon all heraldic and trade-mark rights of the offender enter open usage under the default rules of assumption, so that the first person to assume any such open mark or arms thereafter acquires rights in it.

A person who assumes forfeited arms or marks comes into them and does not become their creator. That person shall state that they acquired them and shall not present themselves as the former bearer.

## ( b ) Intervention by MEF under the Security Policy

1. Using the heraldic arms or blazoned emblem of another within the same jurisdiction without right is a violation subject to Security Policy, section 10. MEF may act either on complaint by an injured bearer submitting reasonable proof (§ ( U/( b ) )) or of its own motion (in the manner of a public prosecutor), particularly where reserved official arms (§ ( U/( ú ) )) are usurped.
2. Where a Prinzian actor asserts copyright in another jurisdiction, MEF proceeds under Security Policy, section 10(e) as set out in § ( B/( u ) ).
3. Every finding, order to show cause, order to comply, and forfeiture declaration issued by MEF takes the form of a signed PDF published on MEF's official platforms under Security Policy, section 10(e)(2).
4. A person who files a complaint with MEF in bad faith is barred from filing complaints with MEF for a term set in the finding.

## ( ú ) Civil Authorities

Pure trade-mark disputes involving unchangeable marks, word marks, domain names, or handles — where no heraldic blazon, reserved MEF emblem, or copyfraud is involved — are civil matters belonging to the competent civil courts of the country in which the parties or the dispute sit, on grounds of impersonation or passing off (civil or criminal), never copyright.
